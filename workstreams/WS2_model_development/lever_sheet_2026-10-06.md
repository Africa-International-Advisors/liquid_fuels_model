# Lever sheet — draft for discussion, 6 October 2026

Prepared by Manish on `manish-branch` for the levers conversation with Nigel.
It defines each lever and sets out the evidence held. It does **not** calibrate
any lever, quantify a scenario or change a model input; the handover reserves
that for after Nigel's review. Proposed cases are starting points to argue
with, not agreed positions.

A lever here is a decision or event outside the model that would move fuel
demand or the import requirement, and that a reader could be asked to take a
view on. Following Nigel's framing in the 6 October stand-up: the base case is
"things continue as they are performing today"; the high and low cases are
what a named decision or event would change.

## A. The levers Nigel named

### 1. Freight moving from road to rail

*What it moves.* Diesel burned by trucks. Rail uses far less fuel per tonne
carried, and much of it is electric.

*Evidence.* Stats SA land transport survey, annual totals from the monthly
series now in the vintage (million tonnes):

| | 2010 | 2015 | 2019 | 2022 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rail | 181 | 219 | 214 | 156 | 161 | 168 |
| Road | 653 | 740 | 895 | 1,049 | 980 | 975 |

Rail lost about 58 million tonnes between 2019 and 2022 and has recovered 12.
Transnet reports 167.9 million tonnes for the year to March 2026 (160.1 the
year before) against a stretch target of 180. The national target is 250
million tonnes by 2029/30: about 185 from Transnet Freight Rail and about 65
from private operators. Eleven operators hold access agreements; their slots
would add 24 million tonnes at first, possibly 52, starting 2026/27. Source for
the Transnet figures is press coverage of its results; its own report was not
retrieved.

*Proposed cases.* Base: rail holds its present share of tonnes. Low fuel
demand: reform succeeds and rail reaches 250 million tonnes by 2030. High fuel
demand: rail falls back to its 2022 level.

*Needed to reach litres.* Tonnes are not tonne-kilometres. The bridge needs the
average haul of the freight that would switch, and diesel per tonne-kilometre
for road and for rail. None is sourced.

### 2. Electric and hybrid vehicle uptake

*What it moves.* Petrol first, then diesel in light commercial vehicles.

*Evidence.*
- Sales: 16,716 new-energy vehicles in 2025, 2.8% of the new vehicle market
  (3.0% in 2024). Of those, 12,818 were conventional hybrids, 2,810 plug-in
  hybrids and 1,088 battery electric (about 0.2% of the market). Hybrids of
  both kinds still burn fuel.
- Fleet: 4,090 electric vehicles registered at December 2023, in a fleet of
  about 12 million.
- Policy: the 2023 Electric Vehicles White Paper aims for a dual platform
  (combustion and electric) in production and consumption by 2035. A 150% tax
  deduction for electric and hydrogen vehicle production assets applies from
  1 March 2026 to 2036; it supports manufacture, not purchase. Imported
  electric vehicles carry a 25% duty against 18% for combustion vehicles.
  Targets quoted alongside the automotive masterplan are 20% of sales by 2025,
  40% by 2030 and 60% by 2035; the 2025 target was missed by a factor of seven.
- Peers (International Energy Agency): electric cars were over 6% of sales in
  Brazil in 2024 and 9% across Southeast Asia, rising to nearly 20% there in
  2025, with Vietnam near 40%. Sales in emerging and developing economies
  reached about 1.2 million in 2025. Chinese imports drive the affordable end.
- The model's current curve (30% ceiling, midpoint 2040) gives 0.2% in 2025,
  which happens to equal the battery electric share, but it has no source.

*Proposed cases.* Base: uptake continues from actual sales, far below the
policy targets, with hybrids treated as efficient petrol cars. Low fuel demand:
purchase incentives or sustained high pump prices bring a Southeast Asian
pattern. High fuel demand: uptake stalls near 3%.

*Needed.* AIA's own view, as Nigel asked: which of the peer paths South Africa
resembles and why. Also fleet turnover: about 0.6 million sales a year into a
fleet of 12.6 million means sales shares take many years to show in fuel use.

### 3. Diesel for grid power

*What it moves.* Diesel burned in Eskom's and independent producers'
open-cycle turbines.

*Evidence.* Turbine output and the litres it implies at the measured 0.31
litres per kWh (three years of reported burn agree on that factor):

| Year to March | Output, GWh | Implied diesel, bn litres |
|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 4,116 | 1.28 |
| 2024 | 5,143 | 1.60 |
| 2025 | 2,838 | 0.89 |
| 2026 | 1,079 | 0.34 |

The implied litres are estimates; the reported figures are for Eskom's own
turbines only. The old workbook's efficiency gives 0.244 litres per kWh.

*Proposed cases.* Base: Eskom's own plan for turbine use. High fuel demand:
load-shedding returns at the 2023–24 level. Low: turbines move to gas or run
for emergencies only.

*Needed.* Nigel's decision on the 0.31 factor; Eskom's forward plan from a
primary document.

### 4. Industry: mining and manufacturing

*Evidence.* Production volume indices (2019 = 100): mining 93.0 in 2023, 93.5
in 2024, 93.7 in 2025; manufacturing 93.7, 93.3, 92.1. Both sit about 7% below
2019 and are flat. The department's balance puts industry diesel at 1.50 bn
litres in 2021, of which mining 1.29.

*Proposed cases.* Base: flat to 2026, then with GDP. High and low follow the
growth cases.

*Needed.* Litres per unit of output. Activity indices are not fuel.

### 5. Agriculture

*Evidence.* Real value added to 2025; balance diesel 1.06 bn litres in 2021.
The model drives it with GDP per person at an unsourced 0.2.

*Needed.* A decision on the driver (value added, planted area or held flat).

### 6. Vehicle fleet growth and use

*Evidence.* Self-propelled vehicles on the register: 11.71 million at December
2021, 12.40 million at December 2025, 12.61 million at June 2026, about 1.4% a
year. Cars 8.34 million, light commercial 2.82 million, trucks 0.41 million.

*Weak points.* The petrol and diesel split by vehicle class is an estimate, and
distance and fuel use per vehicle are 2014 values.

### 7. Economic growth

*Evidence.* Treasury: 1.6% in 2026, 1.8% in 2027, 2.0% in 2028; 2% a year
after, as Manish set on 5 October. Quarterly GDP runs to the second quarter of
2026.

## B. Levers proposed in addition

### 8. Fuel price level

*Why add it.* Regulated inland prices rose sharply in 2026: petrol 95 from
R20.10 a litre in February to R30.25 from 7 October (50%), diesel wholesale
from R17.92 to R31.95 (78%). The model has no response to price, so a base case
built on it implicitly assumes this has no effect on demand.

*Evidence.* Monthly prices to October 2026. One South African study (Boshoff
2012) estimates the long-run price response at about −0.5 for petrol and −0.13
for diesel: a 10% rise in the real price lowers petrol demand by about 5% and
diesel by about 1%. It also bears on electric vehicle uptake (lever 2).

*Proposed cases.* Base: prices settle at a stated real level. High fuel demand:
prices return to early-2026 levels. Low: prices stay at October 2026 levels.

### 9. Private backup generation

*Why add it.* Lever 3 covers grid turbines only. During load-shedding,
businesses and households also ran their own generators, which shows up as
ordinary commercial diesel sales.

*Evidence.* Indirect. SARS put fuel on which levy was declared at 21 bn litres
for the eleven months to February 2025 against 24 bn the year before, and gave
the reason as "increased energy availability". Recorded diesel sales fell from
12.9 to about 11.7 bn litres between 2023 and 2024. Generator imports were reported
up 300% between 2022 and 2023. No measured volume for private generation was found.

*Proposed cases.* Base: no return of load-shedding. High fuel demand: it
returns at 2023 intensity.

### 10. Neighbouring countries' sourcing through South Africa

*Why add it.* Fuel for Botswana, Lesotho and Eswatini moves through South
African ports and terminals. It is throughput for Vopak though not South
African demand.

*Evidence.* SARS records 1.6 to 1.9 bn litres a year exported to nine
neighbours in 2021–2024; the neighbours' own records agree. Botswana's imports
from South Africa fell from 1.02 bn litres in 2023 to 0.63 bn in 2024.
Botswana Oil has said it is diversifying to Walvis Bay and Maputo; historically
about 80% of its fuel came through South Africa.

*Proposed cases.* Base: present volumes. Low throughput: Botswana completes
the shift. This ties to the competing-gateways page of Nigel's pack.

### 11. Domestic refining

*Why it matters.* It sets the import requirement, which is the quantity Vopak's
terminals serve. Nigel's pack treats capacity; these are the events that would
change it.

*Evidence.*
- Output: Secunda, Natref and Astron together reported about 13 to 14 bn
  litres of refined products in 2023 and 2024.
- Clean fuels: petrol and diesel must be at most 10 parts per million sulphur
  from 1 July 2027. Astron is investing up to R6 bn to comply. Natref does not
  yet comply; Sasol has said it will invest. A plant that cannot comply cannot
  sell into the local market.
- Ownership: Sasol operated Natref above its 63.64% share in the year to June
  2026 while its partner was in business rescue.
- New capacity: the state's plan to redevelop the Durban refinery is
  unfunded; press estimates put it above $8 bn.
- Imports already supply more than half of demand.

*Proposed cases.* Base: the three plants keep running and comply. Higher
imports: one plant misses the 2027 deadline or closes. Lower imports: Durban
is redeveloped, no earlier than the early 2030s.

*Needed.* Output by product, which no operator publishes.

### 12. Passengers returning to rail

*Why add it.* Commuter rail collapsed and is recovering; the displaced
journeys moved largely to minibus taxis and cars.

*Evidence.* Rail passenger journeys: 503 million in 2010, 175 million in 2019,
19 million in 2022, 103 million in 2025. Road (bus) journeys moved little: 319
million in 2019, 278 million in 2025. The survey does not cover minibus taxis
or private cars.

*Proposed cases.* Base: recovery continues at its present pace. This is likely
small beside levers 2 and 8; listed so that it is a decision to leave out.

## C. How the levers combine

The model's two scenarios already bundle four inputs: growth, electric vehicle
uptake, efficiency and load-shedding. A fuller matrix, for discussion:

| Lever | Lower fuel demand | Base | Higher fuel demand |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Road to rail | Rail reaches 250 Mt by 2030 | Rail holds its share | Rail back to 2022 level |
| 2 Electric vehicles | Southeast Asian pattern | From actual sales | Stalls near 3% |
| 3 Grid turbines | Emergency use only | Eskom's plan | 2023–24 levels return |
| 4–5 Industry, agriculture | Low growth | Flat, then GDP | High growth |
| 7 Growth | Below Treasury | Treasury, then 2% | Above Treasury |
| 8 Price | Stays at October 2026 | Settles at a stated level | Back to early 2026 |
| 9 Backup generation | None | None | Load-shedding returns |
| 13 Efficiency | Faster improvement | Present rate | Slower |

Levers 10 and 11 do not sit on this axis: 10 is about throughput and 11 about
the import requirement. They would be shown as separate variants.

Levers 3 and 9 move together (both follow load-shedding), as do 2 and 8 (high
prices favour electric vehicles). The matrix should not treat them as
independent.
